## What Nature Portfolio asks referees to cover

The portfolio prompts referees on a fixed set of points. Nature-branded titles ask for key results,
validity, originality and significance, data and methodology, appropriate use of statistics and
treatment of uncertainties, conclusions, suggested improvements, references, clarity and context, and
inflammatory material. Nature Communications uses a variant list that adds analytical approach and
your expertise, and drops originality as a separate heading.

Two consequences worth knowing:

- Reports do not have to follow that order. The portfolio says only that a report should document the
  referee's thought process. Do not assume a report you receive will be numbered or ordered.
- A Nature Communications report more often carries a self-declared expertise boundary, because the
  form asks for it. That changes how much weight an out-of-scope ask from that referee should carry.

Nature Communications also bounds what referees may ask for: suggestions should stay within the present
scope of the manuscript and exclude what would significantly change the scope of the work. Nature-branded
guidance carries no equivalent bound. When you are the referee, respect the bound the journal sets.

Some Nature Portfolio journals expose a separate code-assessment section in the referee form, so a code
comment may arrive outside the main remarks block. Treat it as a first-class part of the report.

Assume your report will be published. Nature has published referee reports and author responses with all
new research submissions since June 2025, and Nature Communications has done so for all research
articles submitted since November 2022. Other titles vary, some by author opt-in. Every referee also
sees every other referee's report and the authors' rebuttal, whether or not the file is published.

Venue choice and article-type fit are not decided here. See `nature-portfolio-playbook`.

## Stage 1: initial assessment

Begin with a high-level evaluation to determine the manuscript's scope, novelty, and overall quality.

**Key questions:**

- What is the central research question or hypothesis?
- What are the main findings and conclusions?
- Is the work scientifically sound and significant?
- Is the work appropriate for the intended venue?
- Are there any immediate major flaws that would preclude publication?

**Output:** a brief summary, two to three sentences, capturing the manuscript's essence and your initial
impression.

## Stage 2: section-by-section review

Evaluate each section, documenting specific concerns and strengths. These checklists are also the ask
taxonomy used by the reply half of this skill: when a received comment has to be classified, it will
almost always land under one of these headings.

### Abstract and title

- **Accuracy:** does the abstract accurately reflect the study's content and conclusions?
- **Clarity:** is the title specific, accurate, and informative?
- **Completeness:** are key findings and methods summarized appropriately?
- **Accessibility:** is the abstract comprehensible to a broad scientific audience?

### Introduction

- **Context:** is the background information adequate and current?
- **Rationale:** is the research question clearly motivated and justified?
- **Novelty:** is the work's originality and significance clearly articulated?
- **Literature:** are relevant prior studies appropriately cited?
- **Objectives:** are research aims and hypotheses clearly stated?

### Methods

- **Reproducibility:** can another researcher replicate the study from the description provided?
- **Rigor:** are the methods appropriate for addressing the research questions?
- **Detail:** are protocols, reagents, equipment, and parameters sufficiently described?
- **Ethics:** are ethical approvals, consent, and data handling properly documented?
- **Statistics:** are statistical methods appropriate, clearly described, and justified?
- **Validation:** are controls, replicates, and validation approaches adequate?

Critical elements to verify:

- sample sizes and power calculations
- randomization and blinding procedures
- inclusion and exclusion criteria
- data collection protocols
- computational methods and software versions
- statistical tests and correction for multiple comparisons

### Results

- **Presentation:** are results presented logically and clearly?
- **Figures and tables:** are visualizations appropriate, clear, and properly labeled?
- **Statistics:** are statistical results properly reported, with effect sizes, confidence intervals,
  and p-values?
- **Objectivity:** are results presented without over-interpretation?
- **Completeness:** are all relevant results included, including negative results?
- **Reproducibility:** are raw data or summary statistics provided?

Common issues to identify:

- selective reporting of results
- inappropriate statistical tests
- missing error bars or measures of variability
- over-fitting or circular analysis
- batch effects or confounding variables
- missing controls or validation experiments

### Discussion

- **Interpretation:** are conclusions supported by the data?
- **Limitations:** are study limitations acknowledged and discussed?
- **Context:** are findings placed appropriately within existing literature?
- **Speculation:** is speculation clearly distinguished from data-supported conclusions?
- **Significance:** are implications and importance clearly articulated?
- **Future directions:** are next steps or unanswered questions discussed?

Red flags:

- overstated conclusions
- ignoring contradictory evidence
- causal claims from correlational data
- inadequate discussion of limitations
- mechanistic claims without mechanistic evidence

### References

- **Completeness:** are key relevant papers cited?
- **Currency:** are recent important studies included?
- **Balance:** are contrary viewpoints appropriately cited?
- **Accuracy:** are citations accurate and appropriate?
- **Self-citation:** is there excessive or inappropriate self-citation?

## Report shape

The shape real Nature Portfolio reports take, and the one to write:

1. Three to six sentences of neutral paraphrase of what the paper does. No criticism yet. This proves
   you read it and it gives the authors a chance to see whether you understood the work.
2. One transition line, for example `The specific comments are as follows:`.
3. Numbered major points, most serious first. If some concerns dominate, say so with a heading such as
   `My biggest concerns are:` and put the rest under a second heading.
4. A figure block, when needed, organized by figure and then by panel.
5. A minor block, each item quoting the manuscript phrase at issue before commenting on it.
6. Remarks on code and data availability, if the form provides a place for them.

## Write comments that can be answered one by one

A report is only as good as the revision it produces, and an author cannot answer a point they cannot
isolate. Every rule below costs you nothing and makes the response checkable.

- **One ask per numbered item.** If a point contains a factual objection and a request for an
  experiment, number them separately. They can be satisfied separately, so they are two points.
- **One object per ask.** `Fig. 2c and 2d both lack units` invites a half-fix that is hard to see.
  Write `c)` and `d)` as separate items. Authors who receive a range routinely update part of it, and
  you will have to catch it in round 2.
- **Number your points.** The portfolio does not require it, but an unnumbered report forces the
  authors to invent labels, and invented labels are where coverage goes missing.
- **Say what would satisfy you.** A point that names the evidence you need is answerable in one round.
  A point that only expresses unease is not.
- **Mark severity.** Distinguish what blocks publication from what would improve the paper. Authors
  spend effort in proportion to what you signal, so an unmarked minor point competes with a real one.
- **Quote before you object.** In the minor block, quote the manuscript phrase, then say what is wrong
  with it. This is what lets the authors fix the right sentence.

When re-reviewing, check the specific objects you named. Referees who list panels, sections, or line
numbers and then verify them individually catch the partial fixes that a general reading misses.
