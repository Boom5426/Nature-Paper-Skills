## Launch principles (`launch-principles`)

The case is the author's nine-principle request (发布会原则, "逐处指出并给出改法") applied to a
synthetic Abstract, Results paragraph and Discussion close for a method that loses the standard
random-split comparison (0.62 against 0.66) and wins on held-out scaffolds, the setting the
Introduction says it was built for (0.48 against 0.31). It also holds attempt narration, grading
words, a parity claim from an interval of -0.08 to +0.10, an untested trade-off reason next to the
measured one, a shuffled-label control and two scope conditions.

The versions separate on the parity item. Both before runs kept a parity claim for a difference of
+0.01 with an interval of -0.08 to +0.10. Both after runs wrote that no difference was detected and
gave the interval. One before run also dropped a scope condition. All four runs kept the
random-split comparison with both numbers, kept the shuffled-label control and stated the
annotation trade-off with both measured changes, so those items do not separate the versions. Both
before runs routed the request as `posture`, and both after runs used the `launch` row.

After 2's failure came from the new rule. Its audit-voice fix said that an attempted variant
becomes an ablation, and the run gave a variant that the case reports without numbers an invented
size.

Every run also added statements that the case does not support, which the rubric does not score: a
gap sentence about current models (4 of 4, against the section contracts), a description of what
the random split contains (3 of 4: before 1, before 2 and after 1) and an untested explanation
stated as established, "shows why" (3 of 4: before 1, before 2 and after 2). After 2 also called
ScaffoldResponse "the only model" that keeps most of its accuracy, although two of the four
baselines have no held-out-scaffold numbers. Before 2 called the random-split gap "close", a grading
word that the rubric does not list, so it passed item 1.

Two lines were added after these runs. The audit-voice fix now asks for an ablation stated with its
numbers, and a variant reported without numbers gains no size. The Launch principles section now
says that principles 6 and 8 add emphasis, not content: a gap, a protocol detail or an explanation
that the supplied text does not give comes from the Introduction or Methods or is left marked, and
an untested explanation is not written as "shows why". Before runs 3 and 4 the expectation was
written down: items 1 to 7 still pass, no run composes a gap sentence or a split description, and no
run states an untested explanation as "shows why".

Runs 3 and 4 met items 1 to 7, and the ablation line had the intended effect: both declined to give
the pathway-prior variant a size because the case reports none. The expectation for the content
line was not met. Both runs composed the same gap sentence ("transcriptional-response models are
rarely tested in that setting"), and both described the random split as giving each test compound
a training neighbour. After 4 wrote that "the ablation shows where that accuracy comes from" and
that withholding annotations "is what buys this transfer", although adding annotations lowers
held-out-scaffold r by 0.07 against a lead of 0.17. After 3 wrote that annotations "reverse this
ordering", which is false: with annotations the random-split r is 0.65, still below 0.66. The line
states the rule but did not change this behavior in these two runs.

Under the case-level rule in `evals/README.md`, where invented evidence or an unsupported mechanism
claim fails a case, none of the six runs passes the case. The item counts show what the launch
rules changed. The unsupported additions are a separate failure that both versions share.

After these runs the skill also asks the reply to list every statement the rewrite adds that the
source does not contain, so that the author sees each one. That change is untested.

## Limits

Two runs per arm before the added lines and two after, one grader, a synthetic case. The grader also
wrote the rule changes and the rubric. All runs ran in an environment whose global instructions were
edited the same day to state the record rule. If the runtime passed those instructions to the
subagents, they applied to both arms. A pass shows that the instructions were followed in these runs,
not that the resulting text is better for readers or reviewers.
